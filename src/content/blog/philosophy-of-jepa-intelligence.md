There's a big philosophical question: do you need words to think? Heidegger, a German philosopher, argued no — he used the concept of Dasein (meaning "being-there") to describe how humans first exist in the world through direct, pre-verbal engagement. You understand a hammer by using it, not by reading its dictionary definition. Understanding comes from being embedded in a situation, not from language about that situation.

This maps surprisingly well onto two AI approaches. Large language models (like ChatGPT or Claude) learn entirely through words — predicting the next token in a sequence. They're powerful but arguably "understand" the world only through its linguistic shadow. JEPA (Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture, developed by Yann LeCun) takes a Heidegger-like approach: it learns by observing raw sensory data — images, video, audio — and building internal conceptual representations of the world without language at all. It predicts what comes next in experience, not in sentences. So JEPA is closer to Heidegger's Dasein — a mind that grasps the world through direct encounter — while language models are closer to what Heidegger would call a derivative, secondary mode of understanding: one that only knows the world through talk about it rather than immersion in it.
